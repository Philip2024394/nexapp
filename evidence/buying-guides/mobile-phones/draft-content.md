## Hero lede

Choosing a phone is less about picking a winner and more about matching a device to the way you actually use one — how long you keep phones, how you shoot photos, where you travel, and how much safety margin you want on software support. This guide walks you through the decisions in order, whether the handset in front of you is new, refurbished, or used.

## 1. Decide what you need the phone for {#what-you-need}

The single most useful question to answer before looking at any spec sheet is: what will this phone be doing on a normal Tuesday in six months' time? A phone used mainly for messaging, navigation, banking and a steady camera roll is a very different purchase from one that has to hold up through heavy mobile gaming, long video shoots, or international travel on multiple carriers.

Consumer Reports' testing programme consistently identifies battery life, display quality, camera performance and overall reliability as the four priorities most buyers end up caring about — not the specs that attract the most marketing (S2). Start from your own workload and work outward from there.

### H3 — A one-paragraph workload audit

- **Daily messaging, email, social media, maps, light photos.** Almost any mid-range phone from the last two to three years handles this; prioritise battery life and software-support length.
- **Serious photography.** Prioritise sensor size over megapixel count, plus the quality of the main wide-angle lens; you will use it far more than the periscope or ultra-wide.
- **Travel across countries.** Prioritise band support (both LTE and 5G) for every carrier you plan to use, and consider eSIM compatibility so you can add local data without swapping cards.
- **Gaming, 4K video capture, on-device AI.** Prioritise current-generation processor, thermal headroom (bigger phones generally dissipate heat better), and 256 GB of storage or more.
- **Long keep-length (5+ years).** Prioritise published software-support end-date and repairability, especially battery access.

### H3 — What usually does not matter as much as it sounds

- Peak brightness numbers quoted by the manufacturer — most are measured in a small spot, not across the whole screen.
- Headline megapixel counts on secondary cameras, which are rarely the sensor you actually photograph with.
- Peak charging-speed watts — above a certain point, all it affects is the last five minutes of a charge.

## 2. New, refurbished or used {#new-refurbished-used}

These three words describe three very different purchases. Reading the label carefully is the first cost-saving step.

A **new** phone is unopened, sold through the manufacturer or an authorised retailer, carries the full manufacturer warranty from the date you buy it, and comes with current software. A **refurbished** phone has been previously owned or returned, then inspected, tested, cleaned and repaired as needed by a seller who graded it and attached some form of warranty (S1, S4). A **used** or **pre-owned** phone has had a previous owner but is sold as-is, with no promise of testing, no refurbishment process, and usually no warranty beyond what remains of the original manufacturer cover (S1).

"Renewed" is not a fourth category — it is a marketplace brand name (notably Amazon's) applied to devices that meet that platform's refurbishment standard. Treat it as a type of refurbished.

### H3 — Refurbished grades, decoded

Grades are **seller-defined**, not legally standardised, so always read the specific definition on the specific listing. Broadly:

- **Grade A / "like new" / "excellent"** — minimal or no visible wear under normal use.
- **Grade B / "very good" / "good"** — light surface marks visible on close inspection; fully functional. Which? has flagged this as the usual balance between price and condition for most buyers (S1).
- **Grade C / "fair"** — visible wear including scratches or minor dents; fully functional.

### H3 — Which route fits which buyer?

- You want the latest features and a full fresh warranty: **new**.
- You want most of the quality of new at a lower price, with a formal warranty: **refurbished from a reputable seller with a published grade and a written warranty of 12 months or more.**
- You want the lowest possible price and are willing to do your own inspection in person: **used**, with the full pre-purchase checklist in Section 4 applied.

### H3 — The warranty question

Manufacturer limited warranties generally transfer with the device because they attach to the serial number and start from the original retail-sale date (S5 for Apple's implementation). Extended-service plans (AppleCare+, Samsung Care+, carrier-insurance programmes) often do **not** transfer, especially if they were on a monthly plan. Before you buy second-hand, check coverage on the manufacturer's online tool using the serial or IMEI.

## 3. Specs that matter, and how to judge them {#specs-that-matter}

Spec sheets are where most buying mistakes get made. A short list of judgements is more useful than a long list of numbers.

### H3 — Display

Independent testing labs such as RTings measure response time, flicker, colour accuracy and motion handling with instruments, not with marketing copy (S14). If display quality matters to you, read independent measurements rather than manufacturer claims. For most buyers, the practical trade-off is size versus one-hand usability, and refresh rate (90 Hz or 120 Hz feels smoother than 60 Hz, but drains more battery when always on).

### H3 — Camera

Sensor size usually matters more than megapixel count. A larger sensor gathers more light, which is what makes low-light and high-dynamic-range photos look better. A 48-megapixel camera on a larger sensor can out-shoot a 200-megapixel camera on a smaller one. Pay more attention to the main wide-angle camera's sensor than to the headline numbers on ultrawide, periscope or depth sensors — the main sensor takes the vast majority of real-world photos.

### H3 — Battery

Battery life depends on battery capacity, display efficiency, processor efficiency and your actual usage pattern — in roughly that order. On a **new** phone, published hours are approximate; independent all-day-use tests tell you more. On a **used** phone, the figure to look at is the battery-health or maximum-capacity percentage shown in the device's own settings — this estimates how much capacity remains versus when the battery was new (S19, S20, S21).

### H3 — Storage

Pick for workload, not habit. A typical cloud-first user on 128 GB is comfortable for two to three years. Heavy local-video or gaming users want 256 GB or more. The operating system and pre-installed apps typically consume 15–20 GB before you store anything (S2). Storage is almost always non-upgradable on modern phones — buy a tier up from your current size if you plan to keep the device three years or more.

### H3 — Connectivity

- **5G band support** — carriers deploy 5G on different frequency bands, and a phone that lists "5G" may not support the specific bands your carrier uses. Compare the phone's published band list to your carrier's coverage page before buying.
- **eSIM** — a programmable SIM profile stored in the phone itself, defined by the GSMA SGP.22 specification (S22). Convenient for travel and adding carriers, but note that a factory reset erases eSIM profiles; a physical SIM retains its identity in the card.
- **Wi-Fi 6E / Wi-Fi 7** — matters if your home router supports it; otherwise irrelevant until you upgrade routers.

### H3 — Build and water resistance

IP ratings are defined in IEC 60529 (S13). The common IP68 rating means the device is fully dust-tight and protected against continuous immersion under conditions the manufacturer declares (usually a specific depth for a specific time in fresh water). This is a laboratory rating on a new sealed device — it does not survive opening for repair, significant drops, salt water, or age. Warranties typically exclude liquid damage even on IP68 devices.

## 4. Checking a used phone before you buy {#checking-used}

If you take nothing else from this guide, take this section. A twenty-minute check in person prevents the most common post-purchase regrets.

### H3 — The five-minute identity check

- **Dial `*#06#`** on the phone and compare the 15-digit IMEI shown to the one printed on the box, SIM tray or in Settings. They must match.
- **Run the IMEI through a lookup service** — the GSMA's Central Equipment Identity Register is the global database carriers submit to (S11, S12); CTIA's Stolen Phone Checker is a US-facing tool fed by that infrastructure (S15). A "clean" result is necessary but not sufficient — BBC reporting has documented that IMEIs can be altered in the grey market (S17).
- **Ask for proof of purchase** from the seller. Not every legitimate seller has kept it, but a seller who refuses outright is a flag.

### H3 — The account-lock check

This is the step most new-to-second-hand buyers skip, and it is the one that kills the most deals after the fact.

- **iPhone:** have the seller factory-reset the phone in front of you, then walk through setup on the spot. If the phone displays an "iPhone Locked to Owner" screen or asks for the previous owner's Apple Account, Activation Lock is still on — do not buy (S5).
- **Android:** same principle, different name. Factory Reset Protection will prompt for the last signed-in Google account after a reset. The seller must pass that screen to a fresh home screen before you pay.

### H3 — The condition inspection

- **Battery health.** iPhone: Settings › Battery › Battery Health & Charging; Pixel: Settings › Battery › Battery Health; Samsung: Settings › Battery › Battery Information (varies by One UI version). 90% or higher is strong; 80% or lower means budget for a battery replacement or negotiate accordingly (S19, S20, S21). A swollen or bulging back cover is a hard "no" regardless of the number — that is a safety issue.
- **Screen.** Display a plain white image to check for burn-in shadows, dead pixels or yellow patches. Display a plain black image to check for backlight bleed and stuck pixels. Open a notes or drawing app and run your finger across the full surface, including corners, to test touch response.
- **Ports and charging.** Inspect the charging port with a torch for bent pins, lint or corrosion. Connect a known-good charger and confirm it reports "charging" within a few seconds.
- **Buttons.** Each button should click crisply with no travel or stickiness — power, volume up, volume down, mute switch where present, and the shutter button on dual-purpose models.
- **Cameras.** Open the camera app, cycle through every lens (wide, ultra-wide, telephoto, front) and take a photo on each. Look for blur, dust specks, or colour casts on one lens that the others don't show.
- **Repair indicators.** On recent iPhones, Settings › General › About shows notices when non-genuine parts have been installed (S6). Treat these as a disclosure point — the phone is still a phone, but the price should reflect the repair history.

### H3 — The red-flag list

- Price well below the market for the model and condition.
- Seller refuses to show the phone powered on.
- Seller refuses to share the IMEI before meeting.
- Payment only via a channel with no buyer protection.
- Any claim that an iCloud lock or Google lock is "easy to remove" — it isn't, and a seller saying this is admitting the phone is locked (S17).

## 5. Software support and longevity {#software-support}

A phone that stops receiving security updates is still a phone, but it stops being a safe banking, email or account-recovery device. Match your intended keep-length to the model's published security-update end date, not to its release date.

### H3 — What manufacturers publish

- **Apple** publishes security-update information per device on its support pages, and in practice iPhones have typically received six to eight years of combined major iOS and security updates (S5).
- **Google** publishes a seven-year commitment of OS and security updates for Pixel 8 and later, measured from first availability on the Google Store in the United States (S7).
- **Samsung** publishes a seven-year commitment of OS and security updates for Galaxy S24 and later flagships, and six years for selected A-series devices from late 2024 onward (S8).
- **Other Android brands** vary widely. Always look up the specific model's published policy; a brand's promise for one tier does not extend to every device it sells.

### H3 — What the EU regulation now sets as a floor

Since 20 June 2025, the EU's Ecodesign regulation for smartphones and tablets has required manufacturers of new phones placed on the EU market to provide operating-system and security updates for at least five years after the last unit of that model is placed on the market, and to ensure spare-parts availability for several years after a model is discontinued (S10). This is a floor, not a ceiling — manufacturers publishing longer commitments go beyond it.

### H3 — Repairability as a longevity signal

Battery health declines with charge cycles regardless of how carefully you use the phone. A phone whose battery can be replaced through an accessible process costs less to keep alive past year three. iFixit's published repairability scoring — validated in peer-reviewed industrial-ecology research — identifies battery access and fastener type as the single biggest drivers of score (S9, S23). Models that score higher are not automatically "better," but they do have a lower cost-of-ownership curve past year three.

### H3 — Treating software support as a line item

When comparing two used phones at similar prices, calculate the remaining security-update window for each. A device with 36 months of support remaining is often a better buy than a slightly nicer device with only 12 months remaining — the cheaper-feeling choice has more usable life.

## 6. Frequently asked questions {#faq}

**What is a "clean" IMEI?**
Every phone has a 15-digit IMEI defined by 3GPP standards (S12). A "clean" IMEI is one that does not appear on the GSMA's global list of handsets reported lost, stolen or with unpaid financing (S11). Blocked IMEIs can be denied service by carriers in the country where they were reported.

**Is an IMEI check enough to confirm a used phone is safe?**
No. IMEIs can be altered in the grey market (S17), and a phone stolen hours before may not yet appear on the list. Combine the IMEI check with in-person inspection, an account-lock test, and a payment channel that offers dispute protection.

**What does "refurbished" actually mean?**
A previously owned or returned device that a seller has inspected, tested, cleaned and repaired as needed, then resold under a published grade with a warranty (S1, S4). "Used" is sold as-is; "renewed" is a marketplace brand name for refurbished.

**How many years of software updates should I expect?**
In the EU, a published minimum of five years is now required by law from 20 June 2025 onward (S10). Google and Samsung publish seven-year commitments for recent flagships (S7, S8); Apple's practical pattern has typically been six to eight years (S5).

**Is a bigger-megapixel camera better?**
Not on its own. Sensor size usually matters more. A large 48-megapixel sensor can out-shoot a small 200-megapixel sensor in low light and dynamic range.

**Does IP68 mean my phone is waterproof?**
No. IP68 under IEC 60529 is a laboratory-tested resistance rating on a new, sealed device — specific to the depth and duration the manufacturer declares (S13). It does not cover salt water, pressurised water, or sealing degraded by age or repair.

**What battery health percentage is acceptable on a used phone?**
90% or higher is strong, 85–89% is acceptable, below 80% means budget for a battery replacement (S19, S20, S21). A swollen back cover is a safety issue — do not buy.

**Does Apple's warranty transfer to me if I buy a used iPhone?**
The remaining portion of the one-year Apple Limited Warranty transfers because it is tied to the serial number and starts from the original retail-sale date (S5). AppleCare+ on legacy single-payment plans often transfers; monthly AppleCare+ does not.

**How do I know Activation Lock or Factory Reset Protection is off?**
Have the seller factory-reset the phone in front of you and walk through setup to a fresh home screen before paying. If any previous-owner account prompt appears, do not buy (S5).

**Should I buy a phone from another country to save money?**
Only after confirming that your destination carrier's 5G bands, VoLTE, Wi-Fi Calling and eSIM support are present on the exact imported model. Some features are region-locked and not switchable.

**eSIM or physical SIM?**
For a single-country single-carrier user, both work fine. eSIM is faster to swap for travel and removes the SIM-tray attack path used in some SIM-swap fraud (S22). A factory reset erases eSIM profiles; a physical SIM retains its identity in the card.

**Is a refurbished phone as good as new?**
For everyday reliability from a reputable seller with a 12-month-plus warranty and a published grade, Consumer Reports' review of the category concludes the gap is usually cosmetic rather than functional (S4).
