# Running Shoes — Choosing a Pair Around Your Running

**Route:** /nex-generated/buying-guides/running-shoes
**Last reviewed:** 2026-11-17

## Hero lede

A good running shoe isn't the one on a "best of 2026" list. It's the one that suits the runs you actually do — the surface under your feet, the distance in your training week, the paces you work at, the shape and width of your foot, and the miles already on your legs. This guide walks through how to choose by the running, not by the brand, and treats the still-contested bits of running-shoe science (pronation matching, drop, super shoes) with the caution the peer-reviewed literature asks for.

---

## Three things to know before you shop

- **Comfort and fit beat theory.** Static arch shape and video pronation sorting aren't well supported as prescriptive tools. Peer-reviewed studies in the British Journal of Sports Medicine have challenged the matching framework used in many retail flows (see FAQ). Fit, width, and a shoe you'll actually want to lace up matter more.
- **No shoe prevents injury.** A well-fitting shoe is part of a sensible setup, but training load, sleep, surface and strength matter at least as much. For persistent pain, speak to a GP, physiotherapist or podiatrist — not to a shoe wall.
- **Stated lifespans are estimates.** The commonly quoted 480–800 km (300–500 mile) midsole life is a rule of thumb, not a guarantee. Heavier runners, hotter climates and single-pair rotations compress foam faster.

## 1. How you run determines what you need — `Footprints`

Before anything about brands or categories, answer four questions honestly: how many kilometres a week, on what surface, at what paces, and with which niggles. Someone running three 5 km loops a week on pavement needs a different shoe from someone training for a hill marathon. The categories most shops use translate roughly like this:

- **Daily trainer** — the workhorse for easy miles, long slow runs and most training. Moderate cushion, durable outsole, nothing extreme.
- **Tempo / workout shoe** — a lighter, more responsive shoe for faster sessions, threshold runs and shorter races.
- **Race-day / plated shoe** — stiff plate plus a lightweight, high-return foam; designed for race pace and peer-reviewed as improving running economy for many runners, but not an everyday trainer.
- **Trail shoe** — aggressive outsole lugs, firmer midsole, reinforced upper, often a rock plate for protection on stony ground.
- **Stability / support shoe** — a daily trainer with structure built into the midsole. Some runners genuinely prefer the feel; the claim that these prevent injury based on arch shape is not well supported (see FAQ Q2).

For most new runners, one well-fitting daily trainer is enough. As weekly volume grows, a second pair — a lighter workout shoe, or a trail shoe if you're heading off road — starts to make sense. One of the few habits with real peer-reviewed support is rotating between pairs: a cohort study published in the Journal of Foot and Ankle Research found runners using several shoes had lower injury incidence than single-shoe runners.

## 2. Fit, shape and sizing — `Ruler`

Fit is the one thing a brand review cannot tell you. Specialty running retailers use variations on the same methodology and it's worth borrowing even if you shop online.

**Length.** Stand up, weight on the foot. Aim for roughly a thumb's width — about 1 cm — between your longest toe and the front of the shoe. Your foot lengthens under load, and it lengthens more on downhills; a shoe that feels snug sitting down will bruise toenails over 15 km.

**Width.** Have both feet measured; most people have one foot bigger than the other. If the forefoot of the shoe compresses your foot, if you get hot spots, or if your toes go numb on long runs, you likely need a wider last or a different brand's shape. Several brands offer wide fits and lasts vary considerably.

**Heel.** The heel should hold without slipping, pinching or blistering. Use the runner's loop — the extra top eyelet most running shoes have — if your heel moves. If lacing can't fix it, the last is wrong for your foot and no amount of foam will rescue the shoe.

**Timing.** Measure feet in the afternoon or evening, with the socks you'll actually run in. Feet swell during the day and on runs; a morning fitting in thin socks is a setup for an under-sized shoe.

**Sizing between men's / women's / unisex.** Women-specific lasts are usually narrower at the heel and softer in midsole density to suit lower average body mass, but there's wide overlap. Many women fit better in a unisex or "men's" last. Fit first, label second.

## 3. Cushioning, drop and stack — `Settings`

Three variables get talked about most — and all three are more nuanced than retail copy suggests.

**Cushioning** is about foam density, thickness and the ride it produces. Softer foams feel forgiving on long runs; firmer foams feel responsive at pace. Neither is objectively "better." Peer-reviewed biomechanics comparisons between maximalist and minimalist shoes show different loading patterns, not a safer category.

**Heel-to-toe drop** is the height difference between heel and forefoot, usually quoted in millimetres. Review-level literature shows drop can shift load between the knee and the Achilles, but there isn't a single "correct" drop. The more consistent finding is that changing drop suddenly — dropping from 10 mm to 4 mm overnight, for instance — is a risk factor on its own. If you're changing drop, change it slowly over months.

**Stack height** is the total foam under the foot. Taller stacks usually mean more cushion and less ground feel; lower stacks are lighter and give more feedback but are less forgiving.

**Plated "super shoes."** A stiff plate plus a lightweight high-return foam has been shown in peer-reviewed work to improve running economy, especially at faster paces. Caveats: they wear faster than daily foams, their ride suits race pace, and their biomechanics differ from a standard trainer. Most runners use them for key workouts and races, not for every easy jog.

If you're new to running or switching categories (minimalist from maximalist, or the reverse), introduce the new shoe gradually: a short easy run once or twice a week before building up.

## 4. Road vs trail vs track — `MapPin`

The outsole is where the surface mismatch bites first.

**Road shoes** have smoother outsoles, softer midsoles and uppers built for heat and mileage. On wet grass or loose stone they slip; on pavement they're what the shoe was engineered for.

**Trail shoes** have aggressive lugs for grip on soil, roots and rock. Many have a firmer midsole, reinforced toe bumper, and sometimes a rock plate to protect the foot from sharp ground. Running pavement in deep-lug trail shoes wears the lugs down fast and makes the ride feel clumpy; running trails in road shoes is slippery and bruising.

**Track shoes and spikes** are for shorter, faster reps and track races. Spikes aren't daily training shoes — the plate is stiff, the upper is minimal, and the fit is race-snug. Most runners doing weekly track sessions use their regular workout shoe and reserve spikes for competition or specific race-prep sessions.

A rough test: if most of your runs are on pavement with the occasional park loop, buy a road shoe and don't overthink it. If more than a couple of runs a week are on soft, stony or technical ground, a dedicated trail shoe earns its place quickly.

## 5. When to replace a running shoe — `Wrench`

Shoes don't fail the way a watch strap does. The midsole foam quietly compresses before the outsole or upper looks finished. Independent editorial and peer-reviewed work generally suggest 480–800 km (roughly 300–500 miles) for a typical midsole, but this is a range, not a rule.

**Faster wear is normal when:**
- You weigh more than average and the foam compresses harder each step.
- You run almost all your miles in the same pair (no rotation).
- Your weekly running is in very hot climates, which accelerates foam breakdown.
- You walk, commute and gym in them too.

**Signs it's time to retire:**
- Midsole foam that stays creased when you press and doesn't spring back.
- Outsole rubber smoothed down to the midsole at your usual contact point.
- A new ache in a familiar run on a familiar route — especially a new calf, shin or knee twinge.
- A fresh pair of the same model feels noticeably different and more cushioned.

Rotating between two or more pairs — one daily trainer plus a workout or long-run shoe — extends the life of each and, per the cohort evidence cited in the ledger, is associated with lower injury incidence.

When you do retire a pair, don't put them back into circulation. Many UK specialty shops and charities run take-back or donation schemes for shoes in good condition; shoes with a collapsed midsole are no longer supportive for the next runner.

## 6. FAQ — `HelpCircle`

**Should I buy a shoe matched to my pronation?**
The simple "overpronator / neutral / underpronator" sort is a popular retail heuristic. Peer-reviewed studies — including work in the British Journal of Sports Medicine — have found that assigning shoes based on static arch shape did not reduce injury rates, and that pronation in novice runners was not a strong injury predictor. The retail framework isn't wrong to offer stability shoes to people who like them; the claim that it prevents injury based on your arch is not well supported.

**How long do running shoes last?**
Typically 480–800 km (300–500 miles) for the midsole, with heavier runners and single-pair users at the lower end. Treat it as a range, not a warranty.

**Do I really need two pairs?**
Not when you're starting. As weekly mileage grows, having a second pair extends the life of each and — per a Journal of Foot and Ankle Research cohort study — is associated with lower injury incidence.

**Are carbon-plated super shoes worth it for a recreational runner?**
For race pace, often yes — peer-reviewed work shows real improvements in running economy. For easy daily miles, usually no — the foams wear faster, the ride suits faster paces, and the biomechanics are different from a normal trainer.

**Can I run while pregnant?**
NHS and ACOG guidance supports moderate exercise — including continuing to run — in most uncomplicated pregnancies, with individual advice from your midwife, GP or obstetrician. Feet often widen and swell during pregnancy, so many runners re-measure and go up in width or half a size.

**My knees hurt after running — is it my shoes?**
Shoes can be part of the picture (worn midsole, abrupt drop change) but persistent knee pain is a clinical question. NHS and Royal College of Podiatry guidance is clear: see a GP, physiotherapist or podiatrist rather than diagnosing from a shoe wall.

**Should I go up half a size for running?**
Many runners do. Feet swell on long runs and in heat. The trustworthy way to find out is to measure late in the day, in the socks you'll actually run in, and keep roughly a thumb's width of space at the toe.

**Are minimalist or barefoot-style shoes safer?**
The "more natural, fewer injuries" claim isn't backed by robust evidence. Switching to very low-drop or minimal shoes abruptly is associated with calf and foot injuries. Some runners transition successfully over months; others never do.

**Can I use running shoes for the gym?**
Walking in them is fine. Lifting and anything with side-to-side motion (court sports, lateral HIIT) is not what a running shoe is built for — they're cushioned for forward motion with an elevated heel, not stable under load.

**My toenails keep bruising — what's wrong?**
Usually length or lacing: the foot slides forward on descents and the toenail bangs the front. Try half a size up, lace the runner's loop to tighten the heel, and check your sock. If it keeps happening, see a podiatrist.

## Seller block — for shops listing running shoes on NEX

If you're selling running shoes through a NEX shop, buyers will look for the fit and use-case information above in your product copy — not just brand and size. Clear, truthful listings help the right shoe reach the right runner. Useful fields to populate honestly:

- **Intended use** — daily trainer / long-run / workout / race / trail / track.
- **Surface** — road, trail, mixed; outsole lug depth if relevant.
- **Stack height and heel-to-toe drop** in millimetres, as stated by the manufacturer.
- **Weight** per shoe at a stated size (UK 8 / US 9 is a common reference).
- **Width options** available (standard, wide, narrow) and the last's typical shape.
- **Condition** if used — approximate km run, visible wear, whether midsole still rebounds. Please don't resell shoes with collapsed midsoles; they're no longer supportive and can contribute to injury.
- **Returns policy** — buyers cannot evaluate running fit from photos alone.

NEX does not rank shops or recommend specific products. We ask sellers to describe shoes honestly so runners can match a shoe to their running.
