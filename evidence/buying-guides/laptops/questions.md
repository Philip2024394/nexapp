# Buyer Questions — Laptops

**Route:** `/nex-generated/buying-guides/laptops`
**Compiled:** 2026-11-17

**Tagging key**
- `[Observed]` — recurring question pattern seen on r/SuggestALaptop, r/laptops, Which? community, or surfaced via People Also Ask style search prompts.
- `[Editorial]` — question the editorial team believes buyers *should* ask but often do not.

Source IDs refer to the research ledger.

---

### 1. How much RAM do I actually need? [Observed]
For writing, email, browsing, and most student work, a mid-tier amount is usually sufficient; for development, creative work, or many open browser tabs, the sweet spot sits noticeably higher; for heavy video editing or large virtualisation workloads, you want a configuration that is comfortably above the sweet spot. Pay attention to whether RAM is soldered — if so, you must buy the capacity you want on day one, because it cannot be upgraded later. Sources: L1, L3, L8.

### 2. Is soldered RAM really that bad? [Editorial]
It is not inherently a failure, but it changes the buying decision. With soldered RAM, you cannot add more in two or three years when your software grows heavier, so the machine's useful life is capped at the capacity you buy now. Slotted RAM allows for a cheaper future upgrade and extends longevity. Sources: L8, L10.

### 3. SSD size versus speed — which matters more? [Observed]
Size matters first: running out of SSD space degrades performance and blocks OS updates. Speed matters second, and mostly when you handle very large files such as raw video, large codebases, or VM images. For everyday workloads, the capacity-versus-headroom balance is more important than chasing peak read/write numbers. Sources: L3, L5, L7.

### 4. What's the difference between IPS, OLED and TN panels? [Observed]
IPS panels give wide viewing angles and consistent colour at a mainstream price; OLED offers per-pixel contrast and deep blacks but can be more reflective and may show static-content wear over long time horizons; TN panels are now mostly found on the cheapest machines and typically have poor viewing angles. Sources: L6, L24.

### 5. Do I need a high-refresh-rate display? [Observed]
For writing, email, and most productivity work, a standard refresh rate is fine. A higher refresh rate helps for gaming and for users who find fast scrolling easier on the eyes. It is not a substitute for colour accuracy if your work is creative. Sources: L6, L7.

### 6. Why do real battery lives never match the quoted ones? [Observed]
Manufacturer battery-life figures are produced under controlled conditions — often local video playback at modest brightness with radios off. Independent measured results for mixed real-world use are consistently shorter. Consult independent testing rather than the box figure. Sources: L2, L5, L7.

### 7. What CPU tier do I actually need? [Observed]
Think in tiers, not SKUs. Low-power mobile tiers (commonly marked U or similarly) suit writing, email, browsing, and most student work with excellent battery life. Mid-tier (often P-class or equivalent) suits developers and creatives on the move. High-performance tiers (H / HX class or similar) suit heavy multi-core work, serious gaming, and video editing — at the cost of battery life and heat. Sources: L11, L12.

### 8. How do I know if a laptop will throttle under load? [Editorial]
You cannot tell from the spec sheet. Thermal behaviour is a function of chassis design, fan tuning, and firmware. Independent reviewers who run sustained-load tests are the only reliable signal. If a machine drops substantially below its sustained rating after a few minutes, your real-world performance is the throttled number. Sources: L7.

### 9. How many years will a new laptop stay supported in software? [Editorial]
Check the OS vendor's support horizon. Windows versions have documented end-of-support dates. ChromeOS devices have a published Auto Update Expiration (AUE) date — buying close to AUE is a false economy. macOS support historically lasts several years but is not published as a fixed date per machine. Sources: L16, L20, L13.

### 10. Is it worth paying for an extended warranty? [Observed]
Statutory consumer rights in the UK and EU already give meaningful coverage for a reasonable period, and some paid extensions overlap with those rights. Read what the extension adds on top — accidental damage cover, for instance, is a genuine extension; "extended warranty" that just duplicates statutory rights is poor value. Sources: L1, L5, L23.

### 11. Should I buy refurbished? [Observed]
Manufacturer-refurbished and reputable-retailer-refurbished units generally carry a warranty, have been tested, and are graded for cosmetic condition. Third-party refurb varies widely. The key risks on a laptop are battery condition, keyboard wear, screen hours, and hinge play — none of which a photo always reveals. Sources: L1, L23.

### 12. How do I check the battery condition of a used laptop? [Observed]
On most Windows laptops you can generate a battery report from the command line that lists design capacity, current full-charge capacity, and cycle count. On macOS, the system report lists cycle count and condition. A healthy battery is one whose full-charge capacity is still well above its design capacity floor and whose cycle count is low relative to the manufacturer's rated cycles. Sources: L9, L25.

### 13. What's a bad battery cycle count on a used laptop? [Observed]
Context matters: different manufacturers rate their batteries for different total cycles. As a rule of thumb, a used laptop with a cycle count that is a small fraction of the rated lifetime is in good shape; one near or above the rated lifetime is on borrowed time and should be priced as needing a battery replacement. Sources: L9, L25.

### 14. How do I check keyboard wear on a used laptop? [Editorial]
Look at the most-used keys — typically the space bar, E, A, S, Enter, and Backspace — for shiny tops, worn legends, or sluggish return. Type a full paragraph; listen for any key that sounds different from its neighbours. A tired keyboard is not always repairable at reasonable cost. Sources: L22, L23.

### 15. How do I check screen hours or panel wear? [Editorial]
Display the same bright, uniform background full-screen and look for uneven brightness, retention, or dead pixels; then a dark background to look for backlight bleed. On OLED, also look for image retention of taskbars or menu bars. There is no universal "screen hours" counter, so visual inspection is the practical check. Sources: L6, L7, L24.

### 16. Why does the hinge matter on a used laptop? [Observed]
A loose, creaking, or stiff hinge is a leading indicator of future screen-cable or chassis damage, and hinge repairs on many thin-and-light laptops are not economical. Open and close the lid several times and listen. Any grinding, catching, or screen flicker during movement is a red flag. Sources: L8, L22.

### 17. Is the Windows licence on a used laptop actually mine? [Observed]
For most OEM laptops, the Windows digital entitlement is tied to the hardware, so the licence transfers with the machine when ownership changes. Check that Windows reports as activated in Settings before you leave the seller. Be cautious with machines sold without any licence state visible. Sources: L15.

### 18. What is Activation Lock and how do I check it on a used Mac? [Observed]
Activation Lock, tied to Find My, prevents a Mac from being set up by a new owner if the previous owner did not sign out of their Apple account. Before buying, have the seller fully sign out and wipe the Mac in front of you, and reach the initial setup screen. If the Mac asks for the previous owner's Apple credentials, do not buy. Sources: L14.

### 19. Can I upgrade RAM later on this laptop? [Observed]
Only if the RAM is slotted. Check iFixit's teardown or the manufacturer's service manual before purchase. Many thin-and-light laptops have soldered memory; most high-performance and some business laptops retain slots. Sources: L8, L10.

### 20. Can I replace the battery myself later? [Editorial]
Varies hugely. Some manufacturers publish replacement guides and sell spares; others glue batteries in and discourage user service. iFixit's device page tells you the difficulty rating before you commit. A machine with a non-replaceable battery is effectively disposable once the cell degrades. Sources: L8, L9, L10.

### 21. What ports do I actually need? [Observed]
Think about everything you plug in on a normal week — monitor, SD card, USB headset, charger, ethernet — and check each is supported natively or via a dongle you already own. Modern thin laptops often have only USB-C; that is fine if you are comfortable with hubs, less fine if you plug into older projectors. Sources: L3, L4.

### 22. Does the keyboard feel matter if I'm mostly a mouse user? [Editorial]
Yes. Even "mostly mouse" users type passwords, URLs, and messages dozens of times a day. A shallow or mushy keyboard that reviewers agree is poor is a slow quality-of-life tax across the laptop's entire life. Try before you buy where possible. Sources: L5.

### 23. Is a 2-in-1 or convertible worth it? [Observed]
Only if you will actually use the tablet or tent modes. The hinge adds mechanical failure points, often adds weight, and sometimes compromises keyboard rigidity. If you are buying it "just in case," a conventional clamshell is usually the better long-term buy. Sources: L3, L5.

### 24. What's different about a Chromebook? [Observed]
ChromeOS is lighter, boots quickly, and is well-suited to browser-based workflows and students. The key buying check is the device's AUE (Auto Update Expiration) date — buy a device with many years of support left, not one near end-of-life. Sources: L20.

### 25. Is macOS worth the premium? [Editorial]
It is a workflow question, not a value question. If your software, peripherals, and colleagues live on macOS, the integration is a real productivity gain. If not, there is no universal performance or longevity argument that forces the decision. Sources: L13, L3.

### 26. Can I run Linux well on a laptop I'm looking at? [Observed]
Compatibility varies by model and generation, particularly around Wi-Fi, fingerprint readers, webcams, and power management. Check whether the manufacturer or the Linux community publishes confirmed-working configurations for the exact model before you buy. Sources: L10, L17, L18.

### 27. Will it game? [Observed]
Light gaming (older titles, indies, 2D) is possible on most mid-tier machines. Modern AAA titles at native resolution need a discrete GPU, strong cooling, and an appropriate display. Thin-and-light laptops that market "gaming capability" without dedicated cooling will throttle under sustained load. Sources: L6, L7.

### 28. What makes a laptop good for video editing? [Observed]
In order: enough RAM to hold your project and previews; a fast SSD with enough capacity for current projects; a CPU and GPU combination that your editor can actually use (check whether it is CPU-bound or GPU-bound for your codec); a colour-accurate display; and sustained thermal performance. Sources: L3, L6, L7.

### 29. How loud and how hot will it get? [Observed]
Only sustained-load reviews answer this. Marketing almost never does. If you edit with the laptop on your lap, surface-temperature readings in independent reviews matter as much as frame rate. Sources: L7.

### 30. What are the biggest buyer traps? [Editorial]
Buying the CPU tier that matches last year's benchmarks rather than your workload; assuming soldered RAM can be upgraded later; trusting manufacturer battery-life figures; buying a used laptop without checking battery cycles, keyboard wear, hinge, licence, and Activation Lock; and buying close to a known software-support end date on Windows, ChromeOS, or an older macOS platform. Sources: L2, L5, L7, L8, L14, L15, L16, L20, L25.

### 31. How do refurb grades (A / B / C) actually differ? [Observed]
Grading is not standardised across retailers. Grade A is typically near-new cosmetically; Grade B shows light marks; Grade C shows visible wear. None of the grades, by themselves, guarantees battery health or keyboard wear. Ask the seller specifically for the battery cycle count and hinge condition in writing. Sources: L1, L23.

### 32. Should I buy ex-corporate / ex-lease laptops? [Editorial]
Business-class chassis are often more repairable and more durable than consumer models and come to the refurb market in volume. They can be an excellent used buy — but confirm the Windows licence state, the BIOS is not locked to the previous owner, and no computrace or corporate asset tag software remains active. Sources: L15, L23.

### 33. Does the manufacturer publish a service manual? [Editorial]
A published service manual, with part numbers, is a strong proxy for long-term repairability. If one exists and parts are available, you can keep the machine running for years longer than one where disassembly is undocumented and parts are pulled only from salvage. Sources: L8, L10.

### 34. Is a bigger screen actually better? [Observed]
A larger screen gives more working area but adds weight, reduces battery life per hour of real use, and may force a less portable charger. Decide based on where you will use the laptop most. If it mostly lives on a desk, a larger panel or an external monitor is a reasonable answer. Sources: L4, L5.

### 35. How long should a new laptop realistically last? [Observed]
With a sensible workload match, slotted RAM, a user-replaceable battery, a vendor who publishes service documentation, and an OS that will remain supported for the duration — many years. Without those, the useful life shortens to roughly the battery's rated cycles or the OS's support horizon, whichever comes first. Sources: L8, L10, L16, L20.
