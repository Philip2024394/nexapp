## Hero lede

A wallet lives in a pocket, so the material and stitching that look great on a product photo have to survive daily flex, card friction, body heat and the occasional wet jacket. This guide explains how to read the small signals — leather grade, tanning type, stitch style, edge finish, RFID claims — so you can judge a wallet on its merits rather than on the brand stamped inside.

## 1. Leather grades decoded (icon: Wallet)

Leather grade is about *which layer of the hide* you are holding. In a wallet, that layer is under constant small-radius flex at the spine, friction at every card slot, and pocket pressure against your hip — so grade matters more than on a bag panel that just hangs.

- **Full-grain**: the outermost surface of the hide, untouched. The fibre structure is at its densest right at the surface — the layer your fingers rub (S3, S8). Patinas rather than peels. Best choice if you want the wallet to age with you.
- **Top-grain**: the top layer, lightly sanded and often embossed with a uniform grain pattern. Slightly less durable than full-grain because the toughest micro-layer has been abraded away (S8). Still a sound wallet material.
- **Corrected-grain**: heavily sanded top layer with an embossed grain and a pigment coating. Looks uniform but the coating can crack along the bifold crease over years (S10). Fine on a flat cardholder; less forgiving on a deep bifold.
- **Genuine leather**: a labelling category under FTC/ISO, not a quality grade — it only means the product contains real leather (S3, S5). Often applied to splits or heavily corrected hides. Read it as the floor, not a feature.
- **Split**: the lower horizontal layer with no natural grain. Usually coated with a polymer finish and embossed. Acceptable for internal panels; weak choice for outer surfaces that flex (S3, S8).
- **Bonded leather**: shredded leather fibre bound with polyurethane and rolled into sheets. FTC guides require the leather content percentage to be disclosed (S5). It delaminates and flakes at folds — avoid in a wallet.
- **Patent leather**: a glossy polymer coating over leather. Water-resistant but the coating is prone to visible crease marks and cannot be refinished at home (S3, S8).
- **Suede**: a split with a raised nap on the flesh side. Stains visibly from pocket oils (S8).
- **Nubuck**: top-grain lightly buffed on the grain side. Stronger than suede but still vulnerable to darkening from skin oils (S8).
- **Vegan / PU / "plant-based" leathers**: polyurethane sheets, sometimes with cactus, mushroom, pineapple or apple fibre content. Often reasonable for a few years of use, but PU hydrolyses over time and typically cracks at fold points earlier than quality leather (S23, S4). Independent longevity data on newer bio-based composites is limited.

> *Context note*: a handbag's leather is judged partly on structure and strap load. A wallet's leather is judged on daily flex at a tight fold radius, friction from card edges, and whether it patinas or peels after a few years of pocket life. Even the same hide can suit one purpose and not the other.

## 2. Tanning: vegetable vs chrome, and what that means in your pocket (icon: Sparkles)

Tanning is the chemistry that turns a hide into leather. Two main systems dominate wallet manufacture.

- **Vegetable-tanned** uses plant tannins (oak, chestnut, mimosa). Produces a firmer, warm-toned leather that darkens and polishes with handling — the patina people associate with craft wallets (S9, S25). Water will mark it; direct heat will crack it; daylight will tan it further.
- **Chrome-tanned** uses chromium(III) salts. Produces a softer, more uniformly dyed leather that tolerates damp and flex better (S9, S25). Holds bright colours. Patinas less dramatically.
- **Combination / retan** leathers start one way and are retanned with the other system; many "pull-up" and "waxed" wallet leathers are combination tans (S10, S11).

Environmental trade-offs are real but nuanced. Chromium(III) tanning is efficient and the fixed chromium in finished leather is biologically inert, but tannery effluent management matters — this is what Leather Working Group audits grade tanneries on (S1, S2). Vegetable tanning is slower and uses natural tannins but requires more water and time. Neither is automatically "green"; the tannery's actual practices and certifications are what to look for.

## 3. Stitching and construction — stress points that fail first (icon: Scissors)

A wallet's stitch line is the chassis. Two things determine how long it holds.

- **Stitch type**. Machine lock-stitch uses one top thread and one bobbin thread looped together — fast and strong, but if the bobbin thread breaks the row can unravel progressively (S18, S19). Saddle stitch uses two needles, one on each side, passing through each hole independently — if one stitch breaks, the stitches either side are still knotted (S19). Hand-saddle-stitched wallets cost more for this reason; machine-stitched wallets are not bad, they just rely on thread integrity more.
- **Stitches per inch (SPI)**. Typical wallet work falls between 7 and 12 SPI (S18, S19). Very low SPI on thin leather leaves visible thread floating between holes; very high SPI on thick leather weakens the panel like a perforation line. Density should suit the leather weight.

**Stress points worth inspecting:**

1. **Card-slot ends.** The top corners of each card pocket take the most wear. Look for a short run of backstitching or a reinforcement stitch there. If stitches end flush at the edge with no backtrack, they can start to unravel after a year.
2. **The fold crease (bifold spine).** A very thick panel stitched tightly at the spine stresses the stitch holes every open/close cycle. Some makers skive the leather thinner at the fold, which reduces this stress.
3. **Zip tape on zip-arounds.** The zip itself is often not the weak point — it's the row of stitches attaching the zip tape to the leather. Dense, even stitching with no visible puckering is what you want.
4. **Interior welt / gusset seams.** If a bifold has an internal gusset (common on long wallets), that seam carries the load when the wallet is loaded with cards and cash.

**Card-pocket construction** matters too. "Skived" means the pocket leather has been thinned at the edge where it meets the shell — this reduces bulk but, if over-skived, can tear. "Folded" (turned-edge) pockets are thicker but hide stress from view. Neither is strictly better; what matters is whether the skive is even and the stitching tracks straight.

## 4. Edge finishing and the small signals of quality (icon: Ruler)

Edges reveal more about a wallet's build than almost any other detail.

- **Burnished edges** are the leather itself, compressed with gum tragacanth or beeswax and polished with a slicker until smooth (S20). You can see the fibre layers continuing right to the edge. Common on veg-tan. Over years they can dry out — a small amount of beeswax paste restores them.
- **Painted edges** are a coat of acrylic or polyurethane edge paint, sometimes several layers sanded between coats (S20). Common on chrome-tan and on mass-market wallets. When well done they are flawless; when poorly done they chip, revealing a raw leather edge underneath.
- **Raw / cut edges** are left exposed, sometimes sealed with a thin wax. Common on craft wallets where the aesthetic is deliberately honest. Easy to inspect: the leather should look dense, not spongy.
- **Turned edges** fold the leather back onto itself and stitch inside, so no cut edge shows. Common on dressy bifolds and long wallets. The downside is you cannot see what is happening at the fold — trust the stitch quality elsewhere.

What to look for in person: consistent edge thickness along the whole panel, no gaps between stitched panels at the corners, and — on painted edges — no visible rings, bubbles or sag at the bottom of the wallet where paint would have pooled during drying.

## 5. RFID shielding — what it does and doesn't do (icon: Shield)

Many wallets advertise RFID protection. Here is the honest version.

- Contactless payment cards use the 13.56 MHz ISO/IEC 14443 standard (S15). A metallised fabric or thin metal layer in a wallet does attenuate that frequency (S14).
- **However**, modern contactless EMV cards generate a one-time cryptogram for each transaction (S15). An attacker who passively intercepts your card's radio emissions cannot replay them to make a fraudulent purchase. This is why reported contactless fraud rates are low relative to magnetic-stripe cloning (S16).
- Where RFID shielding is genuinely useful: **access control cards, transit cards and some corporate ID cards** often use older or weaker authentication, where a copied card number is actually usable (S14, S17).
- It is also useful to prevent **accidental double-reads** at payment terminals when you hold your whole wallet up and two cards are within range.
- **Relay attacks** are a real academic topic (S17) but require specialised hardware and proximity to the card. A shielded wallet does defeat the card-side half of a relay.

So: shielding isn't snake oil, but a shielded wallet is not the single security upgrade some marketing suggests. For EMV payment cards alone the cryptographic protections already do most of the work.

## 6. Care, longevity and end-of-life signs (icon: Clock)

- **Break-in**: just use it. Chrome-tan softens in weeks, firm veg-tan in a few months. Avoid soaking or heavy conditioning on day one (S9, S10).
- **Conditioning**: a light application of a neutral leather conditioner one to three times a year is enough for most wallets. Over-conditioning darkens leather permanently and softens it beyond its design.
- **Wet wallet**: blot with a cloth, open flat, remove cards, dry away from direct heat (S9, S10). Heat shrinks collagen and causes cracking.
- **Cleaning**: a barely damp cloth with pH-neutral soap. Skip baby wipes, alcohol and (on finished leathers) saddle soap — they can strip dye. Suede and nubuck need a dedicated brush, not water.
- **End of life** looks like: flaking surface at the fold (most common on corrected-grain or PU), stitching broken on more than one card slot, edge paint chipping away revealing untreated leather, or card slots that no longer grip.
- **Repair vs replace**: restitching and re-edging a quality full-grain wallet is viable and often economical. Delaminating PU or flaking bonded leather generally cannot be fixed.

**Generic authenticity notes**: real leather at a cut edge shows fibrous layers; PU shows a foam-like cross-section. Real leather carries a faint organic smell; PU often smells faintly chemical. Under FTC and UK consumer protection rules, misleading material labels are prohibited — so always read the fibre label (S5, S6). We do not publish brand-specific counterfeit guides.

## 7. FAQ (icon: HelpCircle)

**Is full-grain always worth the premium?**
For a daily wallet you expect to carry for years, usually yes — the fibre at the surface is the densest and most abrasion-resistant part of the hide (S3, S8). For a secondary or travel wallet, top-grain or a good combination tan is plenty.

**Is "genuine leather" the same as fake?**
No. "Genuine leather" under FTC/ISO vocabulary means real leather, but it is a floor claim — often applied to splits or corrected-grain (S3, S5). It is not, by itself, a mark of quality.

**Should I buy an RFID wallet?**
If you carry access cards, transit cards or ID cards, yes. If you only carry EMV payment cards, it is a belt-and-braces measure — the cards themselves already protect against replay fraud (S14, S15, S17).

**How often should I condition it?**
One to three times a year for most wallets. Chrome-tan needs less than veg-tan. More is not better.

**Why did my new wallet stain my cards?**
Freshly dyed or over-conditioned leather can transfer colour onto adjacent surfaces until it fully sets. Wipe the cards with a dry cloth and let the wallet air for a few days. The transfer usually stops.

**My bifold creaks — is that bad?**
Creaking is common on firm veg-tan and usually quiets as the leather breaks in. Persistent creaking on chrome-tan can indicate the lining and shell are debonding — inspect for a visible gap.

**Can I machine-wash a leather wallet?**
No. Water, agitation and detergent all attack leather. Spot-clean with a barely damp cloth.

**What's the difference between burnished and painted edges?**
Burnished edges are the leather itself, compressed and polished. Painted edges are a coat of acrylic or polyurethane on top of the edge (S20). Either can be done well; the failure modes differ.

**Why does patina darken unevenly?**
UV, skin oils and air exposure all darken veg-tan, and they don't hit the wallet evenly (S9, S25). Rotating how you carry it evens things out over time.

**How many cards should I carry in a bifold?**
Enough that the slots grip but the wallet still closes flat. Chronically overstuffing distorts the slot leather and strains the spine stitching.

**Is thicker leather always more durable?**
No. 1.0–1.6 mm (roughly 2.5–4 oz) is typical for a reason (S8). Thicker adds bulk and strains its own fold stitching.

**When should I replace it?**
When the surface is flaking along the fold, multiple slot stitches have failed, or the edge paint is chipping off onto your cards. Earlier than that is a repair question, not a replacement one.

## For sellers listing leather wallets on NEX

If you're listing leather wallets, buyers now have the vocabulary above. The listings that convert tend to be the ones where the material and construction claims are specific and verifiable.

- State the **leather grade** (full-grain, top-grain, split, bonded, PU) in plain language — don't say "genuine leather" on a full-grain wallet, it sells it short.
- State the **tanning type** if you know it (vegetable, chrome, combination). If a named leather is used (e.g. a Horween leather), name it only if you can substantiate it.
- Say how the wallet is **stitched** (saddle-stitched by hand, machine lock-stitched) and, if you know it, the **SPI**.
- Describe the **edge finish** (burnished, painted, raw, turned) and whether edges are user-reconditionable.
- If you claim **RFID shielding**, be honest about what it protects against — buyers increasingly know the difference.
- Include a close-up photo of a cut edge, a card slot, and the fold spine. Those three images answer most of the questions above.

Accurate material disclosure is also a legal requirement in the US (FTC 16 CFR Part 24) and UK (Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008) (S5, S6). NEX listings should match those standards by default.
